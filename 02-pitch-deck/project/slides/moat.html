Why it is hard to copy

The product is the sauna. The moat is everything around it.

1. A seismic-ready rooftop kit

Steel base for 4.8 kPa occupied roofs, carried to beams and columns. In BC it bolts down with engineered, flashed anchors; in Toronto a ballasted base can pass on SC1/SC2 sites.

2. Canadian certification

Heaters marked cETLus, cULus or CSA to CSA C22.2 No. 164; 208 V design; Ontario’s hard-wired 1-hour timer. Then a CSA A277 factory and a module listing.

3. One permit package, three cities

Engineered once for BCBC, the Vancouver By-law and the Ontario code: 850 mm doors, CSA B651 turning space, a noncombustible cladding option, sprinkler-ready, pool permits for the plunge.

4. The installed base

Every building runs on Vantage Access and Care. Usage data, references and portfolio relationships compound with each roof.

None of these four is a patent. Seismic anchoring is the biggest design change for Canada: in Vancouver and Victoria the code says friction cannot resist earthquake forces on a rooftop component, so a ballast-only base does not pass, and a backyard sauna maker has no answer for that. A US-only ETL or UL mark is not enough in Canada; heaters need a Canadian mark, and buildings with only 600 V service need a transformer. CSA A277 is not mandatory, but in Ontario a certified module is deemed to comply with the code, which saves inspections. Together this is a year or more of engineering, certification and permitting that a competitor would have to repeat, plus the references and data that come only from installed buildings.
